What Women's Sexual Health Issues Can Be Treated at a Specialty Clinic?

A specialty intimacy clinic can evaluate and manage the full range of female sexual health conditions — including low desire, arousal difficulty, painful intercourse, genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and the hormonal imbalances that underlie many of them. These are recognized medical diagnoses with evidence-based treatments, not "normal aging" to be tolerated.

The landscape of women's sexual health concerns is broader than most patients realize, and it spans every decade of adult life:

  • Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD) / Female Sexual Interest and Arousal Disorder (FSIAD): Absent or significantly reduced interest in sexual activity that causes personal distress. This is the most common female sexual complaint across all age groups.
  • Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM): The medical term for the cluster of symptoms — vaginal dryness, thinning tissue, burning, and pain during intercourse — caused by declining estrogen after menopause. The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) estimates GSM affects approximately 50% of postmenopausal women.
  • Sexual arousal disorder: Difficulty becoming physiologically or mentally aroused even with adequate desire and stimulation.
  • Orgasmic disorder: Significant difficulty reaching orgasm, or a marked reduction in intensity, that causes distress.
  • Genito-pelvic pain disorders: Including dyspareunia (pain during intercourse) and vaginismus (involuntary pelvic floor muscle contraction), which are often hormonally and/or structurally driven.

According to landmark research published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (Laumann et al., 1999), approximately 43% of women reported at least one sexual dysfunction symptom in a nationally representative sample. More recent data from the International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health (ISSWSH) confirm that sexual concerns remain highly prevalent across all adult age groups — and that the majority of affected women never discuss them with a healthcare provider.

How Hormones Drive Women's Sexual Health

Hormonal changes are among the most common and most treatable contributors to female sexual dysfunction — and they affect women at every life stage, not just after menopause.

Estrogen supports vaginal lubrication, tissue health, and genital sensitivity. As levels decline during perimenopause and menopause, the tissues of the vulva and vagina become thinner, drier, and more easily irritated — producing the constellation of symptoms now classified as GSM. Unlike hot flashes, which often improve over time on their own, GSM typically worsens progressively without treatment.

Progesterone shifts during the luteal phase and perimenopause can disrupt sleep and mood in ways that compound sexual health issues. And for women who use certain hormonal contraceptives, androgen suppression from combined oral contraceptives is a well-documented contributor to reduced desire that frequently resolves when the method changes — a connection that many prescribing providers fail to discuss.

Systemic hormone therapy — estrogen alone, or estrogen combined with progesterone for women with an intact uterus — remains the most effective treatment for menopausal hormone symptoms, including the sexual health components. Current NAMS guidelines, updated in 2022, support its use in symptomatic women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause onset who have no contraindications, when the benefits outweigh individual risks.

For women who prefer to address GSM symptoms without systemic hormones, local vaginal estrogen — creams, rings, or suppositories — has an extensive safety record and is appropriate for most postmenopausal women, including many who cannot take systemic estrogen. Systemic absorption is minimal. The FDA-approved intravaginal DHEA product (prasterone/Intrarosa) and the oral selective estrogen receptor modulator ospemifene (Osphena) are additional non-systemic options for dyspareunia associated with GSM.

Low-Dose Testosterone for Women: The Evidence-Based Option Few Providers Offer

Testosterone is not just a male hormone — women produce it too, in lower amounts, and it plays a meaningful role in libido, energy, mood, and arousal. Testosterone levels in women decline steadily throughout adult life, accelerating around surgical menopause and often falling to low-normal or below-normal ranges by the mid-forties, independent of whether a woman has reached menopause.

The 2019 Global Consensus Position Statement from ISSWSH — endorsed by the British Menopause Society, European Menopause and Andropause Society, and ten other major professional societies — reviewed the evidence and concluded that testosterone therapy for postmenopausal women with HSDD is supported by Level I evidence from multiple randomized controlled trials. The statement found no signal of serious safety concerns at physiologic doses and affirmed that low-dose testosterone produces clinically meaningful improvements in desire, arousal, orgasm frequency, and overall sexual satisfaction.

The catch: no testosterone formulation is currently FDA-approved in the United States specifically for women. It is prescribed off-label using formulations calibrated to female physiologic ranges — far lower doses than those used in men. The absence of an approved product reflects regulatory history more than evidence gaps; the evidence for efficacy and safety at appropriate doses is well-established in the peer-reviewed literature.

For women whose testosterone is low-normal or below range and who report HSDD, low-dose testosterone therapy may be part of the conversation — alongside a discussion of the evidence, the off-label status, and what ongoing monitoring involves.

Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause: The Most Underdiagnosed Condition in Women's Health

GSM deserves its own section — not because it is the most severe condition we treat, but because it is so consistently undertreated despite being so common and so responsive to therapy.

The symptoms of GSM — vaginal dryness, burning, irritation, pain during or after intercourse, and sometimes urinary urgency and recurrent UTIs — affect roughly half of postmenopausal women according to NAMS. Yet surveys consistently show that fewer than 25% of affected women have discussed these symptoms with a clinician, and a smaller fraction still have been offered treatment.

Part of the problem is language: many women describe these symptoms as "just being dry" and don't connect them to a treatable medical condition. Part of the problem is that routine well-woman visits rarely allocate time for sexual health conversation. And part of the problem is that over-the-counter lubricants, while useful for symptom management, do not address the underlying tissue atrophy — so the symptoms gradually worsen even when women feel they're managing them.

Effective treatments exist. Local vaginal estrogen, intravaginal DHEA, and ospemifene are all FDA-approved specifically for symptoms of GSM and have robust clinical trial support. For women whose symptoms are significantly affecting quality of life or relationship health, these treatments can produce meaningful relief within weeks of starting.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes low sex drive in women?

Low libido in women is almost always multifactorial. Hormonal causes are among the most common — declining estrogen and testosterone during perimenopause and menopause, postpartum hormonal shifts, and the libido-suppressing effect of many combined hormonal contraceptives. Psychological contributors include stress, anxiety, depression, and relationship factors. Medical causes include certain medications (especially SSRIs, antihistamines, and some blood pressure drugs), chronic illness, pelvic pain, and thyroid dysfunction. A comprehensive evaluation that considers all of these — not just one — is the starting point for effective care.

Is there a medication equivalent to Viagra for women?

There is no exact equivalent, but there are FDA-approved options. Flibanserin (Addyi) and bremelanotide (Vyleesi) are both FDA-approved for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women. For postmenopausal women, local and systemic hormone therapies, ospemifene (Osphena), and intravaginal DHEA (Intrarosa) are established options. Low-dose testosterone, while not FDA-approved specifically for women in the United States, is endorsed by major medical societies including ISSWSH and NAMS for postmenopausal women with low libido and has strong clinical trial support.

What is the best treatment for vaginal dryness and discomfort after menopause?

Local vaginal estrogen — available as creams, rings, or suppositories — is one of the most effective and well-established treatments for genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM). Because systemic absorption is very low, it is appropriate for most postmenopausal women, including many who cannot use systemic hormone therapy. Intravaginal DHEA (Intrarosa) and ospemifene (Osphena) are effective alternatives. A clinician can help determine which is most appropriate based on your symptoms and health history.

Is testosterone therapy safe for women?

At the low physiologic doses used in women's sexual health treatment, testosterone has a well-established safety record supported by multiple randomized controlled trials and endorsed by the ISSWSH and NAMS. No FDA-approved testosterone formulation is licensed specifically for women in the United States, so it is prescribed off-label. Risks at appropriate doses are low; monitoring by an experienced provider is standard practice and important for safety.

What is genitourinary syndrome of menopause?

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is the current medical term for the symptoms caused by declining estrogen in the vulvar, vaginal, and urinary tissues after menopause: vaginal dryness, irritation, burning, pain during intercourse, urinary urgency, and recurrent urinary tract infections. NAMS estimates GSM affects approximately 50% of postmenopausal women. Unlike hot flashes, GSM typically does not resolve on its own and often worsens over time without treatment — which is why it is important to seek care rather than wait it out.

Can a regular OB-GYN treat women's sexual health concerns?

Some can, but sexual medicine is a subspecialty, and many OB-GYNs and general practitioners receive limited training in female sexual dysfunction beyond basic gynecology. Patients often report feeling rushed or dismissed when raising intimacy concerns during routine appointments. A clinic that specializes in sexual health and hormone optimization — and structures appointments around thorough evaluation including comprehensive hormone panels — can offer more comprehensive and individualized care than a standard well-woman visit.

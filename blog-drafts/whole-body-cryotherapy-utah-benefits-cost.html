Whole Body Cryotherapy in Utah: Benefits, Cost, and What to Expect

Three minutes. That's how long it takes. You step into a chamber, the temperature drops to around -120°C (-184°F), and for the next two to three minutes your body works hard to protect itself from the cold. When you step back out — warmer room, gloves off, heart rate settling — many clients report something unexpected: a surge of energy and a lightness they didn't walk in with.

Whole-body cryotherapy has moved from elite sports recovery into mainstream wellness, and Utah has a growing number of facilities offering it. But what does the research actually say? What does a session cost? Who genuinely benefits — and who shouldn't use it? This guide answers all of it, grounded in peer-reviewed sources, with clear safety information and no overpromising.

What Is Whole-Body Cryotherapy?

Whole-body cryotherapy (WBC) exposes your entire body to dry, ultra-cold air — typically between -110°C and -140°C (-166°F to -220°F) — for two to three minutes inside a temperature-controlled chamber. The brief cold exposure triggers a physiological survival response that researchers have studied for effects on inflammation, post-exercise recovery, pain management, and mood. Important upfront: WBC is an emerging wellness modality, not an FDA-approved medical treatment for any condition.

The proposed mechanism works like this: when your skin surface temperature plummets, your body responds by rapidly pulling blood from your extremities toward your core organs — a process called vasoconstriction. Stress hormones including norepinephrine surge (research has documented plasma increases of 200–300%). When you step back into normal air and the body re-warms, that oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood redistributes back to muscles, joints, and skin. This reversal — vasodilation following vasoconstriction — is thought to drive the recovery and anti-inflammatory effects researchers have documented.

WBC differs from a cold plunge or ice bath in one practical way: the cold medium is dry air, not water. Because water conducts heat away from the body far more aggressively than cold air, most people find a cryochamber more tolerable than equivalent-temperature immersion. Sessions are brief by design — clinical research protocols use 2–3 minutes, and no current safety guideline recommends exceeding five minutes.

What the Research Actually Shows

Peer-reviewed evidence is strongest for systemic inflammation reduction and post-exercise recovery. A March 2025 meta-analysis in Scientific Reports (a Nature Publishing Group journal) analyzed 11 randomized controlled trials with 274 total participants and found WBC significantly reduced the pro-inflammatory marker IL-1β while raising the anti-inflammatory IL-10 — with the strongest effects observed in athletes and individuals with obesity. Evidence for pain relief and mood improvement is also meaningful. Evidence for direct fat loss is preliminary and should not be overstated.

Inflammation reduction. Beyond the 2025 meta-analysis (PubMed ID 40044835), a 2024 pilot cohort study published in the Interactive Journal of Medical Research (PMC11624452) followed 15 healthy adults over approximately nine months of regular WBC. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) — a gold-standard systemic inflammation marker — fell from a mean of 3.39 mg/L at baseline to 1.88 mg/L at follow-up (p = 0.03), a statistically significant reduction. Researchers calculated an average reduction of 0.14 mg/L hsCRP per session, with the effect sustained across the nine-month observation period. The study also noted a trend toward lower fasting glucose.

Exercise recovery. Multiple systematic reviews and a 2025–2026 network meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living compared WBC to other post-exercise recovery modalities. WBC performed comparably to cold-water immersion for delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) in most comparisons, with a possible advantage for jump-height recovery. A separate single-session study (PMC9895013) found that one WBC session enhanced maximal cycling performance and improved markers of parasympathetic (rest-and-recovery) nervous system activity.

Pain relief. A study of 30 patients with lumbar spine osteoarthritis (PMC9494794) found that 10 WBC sessions at -130°C over two weeks — combined with therapeutic exercise — produced statistically significant pain reduction on the Visual Analog Scale (p < 0.0001), alongside elevated beta-endorphin levels. In fibromyalgia, a published randomized clinical trial (PMC6223856) demonstrated beneficial effects on symptom scores. Pooled data from multiple studies in rheumatoid arthritis showed reduced pain and disease activity, though results were not always significantly greater than physical rehabilitation alone.

Mood and mental health. A randomized controlled trial in Frontiers in Psychiatry (PMC7296110) studied WBC as an add-on therapy to pharmacological treatment of depression and found statistically significant improvements in depression rating scales. A 2021 meta-analysis of six eligible RCTs described WBC as "an effective add-on treatment for mental health problems, particularly depression."

An important FDA note. Whole-body cryotherapy is not approved by the FDA for any medical condition. The FDA has stated it found "very little evidence about its safety or effectiveness" for the conditions cryotherapy is commonly marketed to treat, and has flagged real risks: frostbite, burns, eye injury, and asphyxiation risk when liquid nitrogen units are used without adequate ventilation. Understanding this regulatory context is essential: the studies above show WBC produces measurable physiological effects — particularly on inflammation and recovery — but it remains a wellness modality, not a regulated medical treatment.

What to Expect at Your First Session

A first cryotherapy appointment takes roughly 10–15 minutes from check-in to exit — a brief health intake, a few minutes to change and put on protective gear, and the 2–3-minute session itself. Most clients describe the sensation as an intense, dry cold that is more manageable than expected, followed by a noticeable warmth and energy lift within minutes of stepping out.

  • Before: Complete a medical history and waiver form. Staff will review contraindications. You'll change into minimal clothing — shorts for men; swimwear or shorts and a sports bra for women. Protective gear provided includes dry socks, slippers, gloves, and an ear or head covering. All jewelry and any moisture on the skin must be removed before entering.
  • During: Enter the cryochamber. In nitrogen-gas sauna units, your head remains above the opening and breathes normal room air. In electric whole-body units, the entire body is enclosed with filtered air supplied inside. Staff supervise throughout. The air is extremely cold but dry — most describe it as "sharp" rather than painful, and 2–3 minutes passes quickly.
  • Immediately after: Skin temperature normalizes within minutes of exiting. The most commonly reported immediate effects are an energy surge, mood lift, and light tingling on the skin. In pain-condition research, roughly 40% of patients reported complete pain relief immediately after WBC with an additional 50% reporting partial relief — though those figures come from specific clinical populations, not the general wellness user.
  • In the following 24–48 hours: Anti-inflammatory and muscle soreness reduction effects are typically most notable in this window. Many regular users also report improved sleep quality, which some researchers attribute to the post-session parasympathetic rebound.

Clinical research protocols typically use 10–20 sessions to demonstrate measurable, sustained effects — which is why single-session experiences vary and why package programs tend to align better with both the evidence and the long-term value proposition.

How Much Does Cryotherapy Cost in Utah?

A single whole-body cryotherapy session in Utah typically runs $40 to $100, consistent with national pricing. Package deals — usually 5–10 sessions for $200–$600 — reduce the per-session cost substantially, which matters because clinical research protocols use repeated sessions to produce measurable, sustained effects; a single session rarely matches the protocols in the published literature.

  • Single session: $40–$100 per 2–4-minute session. First-time promotions at some Utah facilities can bring introductory pricing below $40.
  • Package pricing: $200–$600 for 5–10 sessions nationally and in the Utah market. The per-session cost drops meaningfully at this tier.
  • Research-aligned frequency: The 2024 hsCRP pilot study participants averaged approximately seven sessions per month over nine months to achieve and maintain significant inflammation reductions. Occasional individual sessions may produce short-term recovery benefits; the sustained anti-inflammatory effects in the published research came from regular, ongoing participation.

Insurance coverage: Cryotherapy is not covered by health insurance for most applications, as it is not FDA-approved for medical conditions. Some patients use HSA or FSA funds when a physician has recommended WBC as part of a care plan — eligibility is plan-specific, so verify with your benefits provider before assuming coverage.

Who Should Not Try Cryotherapy?

Cryotherapy is not appropriate for everyone, and the contraindication list is extensive. A 2025 position paper from the International Institute of Refrigeration (IIR) — developed by a multidisciplinary expert panel using Delphi consensus methodology and published in Frontiers in Rehabilitation Sciences (PMC12037594) — provides the most current and comprehensive safety guidance available. Key permanent contraindications include:

  • Cardiovascular: Ischemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension (≥160/100 mmHg), decompensated heart failure, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pacemakers or implantable defibrillators, peripheral artery disease (stages 2–4), recent pulmonary embolism (within six months), uncontrolled arrhythmias (heart rate >110 bpm), aortic dilatation ≥45 mm
  • Metabolic/Endocrine: Type 1 Diabetes, uncontrolled Type 2 Diabetes with vascular or retinal complications, uncontrolled thyroid disease, hypoadrenalism
  • Oncological: Active or untreated cancers, malignant melanoma, ongoing chemotherapy or radiation
  • Pulmonary: Severe asthma, COPD stages III–IV, active tuberculosis
  • Neurological: Epilepsy, recent stroke (within 12 months), dementia, significant polyneuropathies
  • Other: Pregnancy, cryoglobulinemia, Raynaud's phenomenon, severe renal insufficiency (stages III–IV), age over 80

Temporary deferral conditions — active infection or fever, blood pressure above 160/100 mmHg, acute illness, or excessive skin moisture — mean a session should be postponed until those conditions resolve. If you have any cardiovascular condition, metabolic disease, neurological disorder, or chronic illness, speak with your physician before booking a session.

Can Cryotherapy Support Your Weight Loss or Wellness Goals?

No peer-reviewed clinical trials have directly combined WBC with GLP-1 medications or hormone therapy programs. That is the honest starting point. However, cryotherapy's documented effects on systemic inflammation and post-exercise recovery make it a clinically reasonable complement to medical weight loss and hormone optimization — not as a replacement for those treatments, but as a supportive wellness modality that works on related physiology.

For medical weight loss: GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide reduce systemic inflammation as one of their broader metabolic effects. WBC independently reduces hsCRP and pro-inflammatory cytokines through a separate mechanism. Beyond that direct overlap, better post-exercise recovery means you can train more consistently — and consistent resistance training is essential for preserving lean muscle mass during GLP-1-assisted weight loss, which carries a known muscle-loss risk. Cold exposure may also stimulate brown adipose tissue (brown fat) activity and influence thermogenesis — though the calorie-burn claims circulating in wellness marketing (commonly cited as 200–800 calories per session) lack peer-reviewed precision and should be treated with appropriate skepticism. If you're exploring a medically supervised GLP-1 weight loss program, cryotherapy may fit as a complementary wellness support, not a standalone intervention.

For hormone optimization: Chronic systemic inflammation suppresses testosterone production — one reason men with elevated inflammatory markers sometimes have suboptimal testosterone levels even without a primary hormonal disorder. If WBC reduces inflammation as the research suggests, it may support the hormonal environment that testosterone optimization works to improve. Better post-exercise recovery also supports the body composition goals men typically seek from TRT. These are mechanistically plausible connections — not claims validated by direct clinical trials combining WBC and TRT.

The most practical framing: cryotherapy is a wellness tool with a genuine evidence base for inflammation and recovery. It is not a medical treatment and won't replace care for underlying conditions. As part of a broader program that includes regular exercise, proper nutrition, and medically supervised treatment when indicated, it adds a physiologically grounded element to your routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does whole-body cryotherapy actually work?

For inflammation reduction and post-exercise recovery, peer-reviewed research — including a March 2025 meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials published in Scientific Reports — shows measurable, statistically significant effects. For pain relief in specific conditions such as low back pain and fibromyalgia, evidence is meaningful. For mood improvement, at least one RCT showed benefit as an add-on therapy in depression treatment. Evidence for direct weight loss from WBC alone is preliminary at best. The FDA has not approved WBC for any medical condition and has noted limited evidence for the disease-treatment claims often used in marketing.

How many cryotherapy sessions do you need to see results?

Single-session benefits — the post-session energy surge and mood lift — are reported widely and may be experienced from the very first visit. For sustained inflammation reduction, the best available research involved an average of roughly seven sessions per month over nine months to achieve and maintain significant hsCRP reductions. Most clinical research protocols used 10–20 sessions to demonstrate measurable outcomes. Package programs that support regular, ongoing use align better with the evidence base than occasional single sessions.

Is cryotherapy safe?

For healthy individuals who clear contraindication screening, WBC is generally well-tolerated. The most common side effects are mild skin tingling and temporary blood pressure elevation that resolves with re-warming. Serious risks — frostbite, burns, asphyxiation in improperly ventilated nitrogen-gas units — are associated with equipment failure or unsupervised use. The FDA has issued consumer warnings about these hazards. Anyone with cardiovascular disease, a pacemaker, Type 1 Diabetes, active cancer, severe asthma, epilepsy, or recent stroke should not undergo WBC. Consult your physician before starting, particularly if you have any chronic health condition.

Does cryotherapy help with weight loss?

Not directly in any clinically proven, meaningful way. Cryotherapy may activate brown adipose tissue, support more consistent training through better recovery, and reduce systemic inflammation associated with obesity — but no peer-reviewed study has demonstrated meaningful direct fat loss from WBC as a standalone intervention. Commercial claims about calorie burn per session (widely cited at 200–800 calories) lack peer-reviewed validation. WBC may complement a comprehensive medical weight management program; it is not itself a weight loss treatment.

Can I combine cryotherapy with other wellness treatments?

Generally yes, for healthy individuals without contraindications. WBC pairs naturally with exercise recovery protocols, and there is mechanistic logic to combining it with anti-inflammatory wellness programs. No peer-reviewed studies have examined WBC specifically in combination with GLP-1 medications or hormone therapy — claims about synergistic effects in those specific contexts should be approached with appropriate caution. Discuss any planned combination with your healthcare provider, particularly if you have conditions that require medical management.

Is cryotherapy covered by insurance?

Typically no. Because WBC is not FDA-approved for medical conditions, most health insurance plans do not cover it. Some patients use HSA or FSA funds when their physician has recommended WBC as part of a documented care plan — eligibility varies by plan, so verify with your benefits provider. Cryotherapy is not currently covered by Medicare or Medicaid for standard wellness applications.

What should I look for in a cryotherapy facility?

Look for: thorough contraindication screening before your first session; trained, certified staff supervising every session; properly maintained equipment with documented temperature calibration; and clear safety protocols including emergency procedures. Any reputable facility will walk through your health history, review the contraindication list, and explain the safety gear before you enter the chamber. If a facility skips this step, that is a red flag worth taking seriously.
